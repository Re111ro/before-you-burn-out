✦ OCCUPATIONAL BURNOUT AWARENESS

Burnout doesn't happen overnight.

Sometimes the first warning signs are easy to ignore. Learn how long-term workplace stress affects people, why it happens, and what employees and workplaces can do about it.

01 / UNDERSTAND

It's more than being tired.

The World Health Organization (WHO) describes burnout as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It is an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. WHO describes three main dimensions:

▰

1. Exhaustion

Feeling drained of energy and emotionally worn out by work.

Example: "I have no energy left after my workday."

☁

2. Cynicism or distance

Feeling increasingly negative about work or mentally disconnected from it.

Example: "I used to care about this job much more."

↘

3. Reduced efficacy

Feeling less effective or capable in your professional role.

Example: "Nothing I do seems good enough."

Based on WHO's ICD-11 description of burnout ↗

02 / EARLY AWARENESS

The warning signs we ignore.

Burnout is connected to ongoing work stress. Its core dimensions are exhaustion, distancing from work and reduced professional efficacy. Other changes below may also occur with stress, but none of these signs alone proves that someone has burnout.

♡

Feelings

  • Feeling exhausted by work
  • More frustration about work
  • Feeling detached or unmotivated
✎

Focus & behavior

  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Feeling overwhelmed by tasks
  • Feeling less effective at work
◔

Physical strain

  • Feeling unusually tired
  • Sleep difficulties
  • Tension or headaches
Normal tiredness or something more?

Temporary tiredness can follow a busy day. Burnout, by contrast, is linked to persistent workplace stress and a wider pattern of changes in energy, attitude toward work and professional effectiveness. Look for a continuing pattern, not one difficult day.

Sources: WHO ↗ and Maslach & Leiter (2016) ↗. The additional symptoms are possible stress-related experiences, not WHO diagnostic criteria.

03 / WHY IT HAPPENS

Sometimes the problem is the workplace.

Researchers Christina Maslach and Michael Leiter have studied how the fit between a person and their working conditions can contribute to burnout. Their six areas of worklife help us think about where problems may begin.

▤

Workload

Too much work without enough time or resources.

Example: Urgent deadlines keep piling up.

☷

Control

Little opportunity to influence tasks or decisions.

Example: Every task is assigned with no flexibility.

☆

Reward

Too little recognition or appreciation for effort.

Example: Good work is consistently overlooked.

♡

Community

Weak support, isolation or conflict with coworkers.

Example: Nobody feels safe asking for help.

⚖

Fairness

Unfair treatment or inconsistent decisions.

Example: Work is distributed unequally.

✧

Values

Conflict between personal values and job expectations.

Example: Workers are pressured to ignore their standards.

Why early awareness matters

Recognizing patterns is useful, but awareness is only a first step. For example, if heavy workloads are the problem, an organization may need to review staffing, priorities or deadlines—not simply tell workers to rest more.

Based on Maslach & Leiter's research ↗ and HSE's workplace-stress guidance ↗. All examples are fictional.

04 / WHAT HELPS

Prevention takes more than a break.

The WHO emphasizes improving workplace conditions as well as supporting individual wellbeing. Different people have different responsibilities in preventing harmful work stress.

☕

Employees

  • Notice patterns that repeatedly drain your energy.
  • Ask for clarity on tasks and priorities.
  • Protect rest time where possible.
  • Seek appropriate support when stress persists.
☏

Managers

  • Listen seriously to concerns.
  • Set realistic deadlines and workloads.
  • Make responsibilities clear.
  • Encourage respectful communication.
✿

Organizations

  • Identify risks in the work environment.
  • Improve job control and support.
  • Review unfair or excessive demands.
  • Involve employees in useful changes.
“Better wellbeing needs better working conditions, too.”Our summary of WHO workplace mental-health guidance — not a direct quotation.
